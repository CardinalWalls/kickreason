# The Intel Desk — how we hold ourselves

The expert mindset, codified. An amateur gives a hot take; a pro puts a number on it, names its sources,
argues the opposite, and keeps score out loud. These translate the formal intelligence rulebooks (ICD 203,
Heuer's Analysis of Competing Hypotheses, Sherman Kent, Tetlock, the NATO Admiralty source code) into plain
desk rules.

## The operating standard
1. **Put a number on it, not a word.** "Good chance" means 75% to one reader and 30% to another (Kent, 1964).
2. **Keep "how likely" and "how sure" apart.** Likelihood (the event) ≠ confidence (your evidence). Print them
   separately (ICD 203).
3. **Rate every source** — the source AND the specific claim, separately. A trusted name doesn't launder a
   weak fact.
4. **"Confirmed" means independent, not louder.** Echo is not corroboration.
5. **Argue the other side.** List the plausible outcomes first, then ask of each fact: which does it rule OUT?
   (Heuer's ACH — note: it buys legibility, not accuracy.)
6. **Use all the feeds, and name the gap.** Say plainly what you couldn't see.
7. **Lead with the answer, then show the chain** — and surface the contrary evidence, don't bury it.
8. **Score yourself out loud, and say what moved the number.** A time-stamped, calibrated track record
   (Tetlock's superforecasters beat vague experts). When news moves a number, the diff *is* the explanation.

**Anti-rule:** don't hedge to stay safe. A cowardly 50/50 to avoid being wrong is its own failure
(ICD 203 Std 8). Commit to the hard call; let the scorecard judge it.

## House style for a published forecast
A **number** + a **band word** (one fixed lexicon) + a **range** + a separate **confidence** + the
**sources** + **what would change our mind**. Example:
> Argentina to beat Mexico (group): **likely, ~70% (range 60–80%). Confidence: moderate.** Stronger on the
> Elo/SPI baseline; market agrees. Moderate — XI unconfirmed. What would change it: Messi confirmed rested.
